Music of the Deccan

Qawwali

Sufi Devotional Singing · The Deccan Dargahs

Religious settingSufi samāʿ and shrine assemblies[1]
Early Deccan centreGulbarga under the Bahmanis[1]
Key shrineGisu Daraz / Bandanawaz at Gulbarga[2]

Qawwali in the DeccanSamāʿ◆Bahmani Patronage◆Gulbarga◆Court & Shrine

Qawwali in the Deccan belongs first to the history of Sufi samāʿ — devotional listening assemblies in which poetry and music were used as vehicles of remembrance and spiritual attention. The form travelled south with Sufi networks and became embedded in the religious and courtly culture of the Bahmani Deccan.[1]

I · The PracticeSufi Samāʿ

Music as devotional listening

Qawwali is better understood through the Sufi assembly than through the modern concert stage

Historical discussions of the Chishti tradition describe samāʿ as an organized devotional practice centred on sung poetry, attentive listening and spiritual response. Qawwals were specialist singers within that setting; the performance was not simply entertainment added to a shrine visit but part of the devotional culture of the order.[3]

That context helps explain why qawwali travelled with Sufi lineages. When Chishti teachers and disciples established themselves in the Deccan, they carried not only doctrine and institutional forms but also a culture of sung Persian and vernacular poetry.

II · Court PatronageBahmani Deccan

Qawwals at the Bahmani court

The documentary record connects Sufi musical assemblies with royal patronage

Encyclopaedia Iranica’s account of the Bahmanids notes that qawwali was a regular feature of Sufi assemblies in the Deccan and that Persian ghazals were commonly sung. It also records that Bahmani ruler Muhammad I invited qawwals and musicians from Delhi, including performers of poetry by Amir Khusrau and Hasan Sijzi.[1]

This is stronger evidence than a generic claim that “the court liked music.” It shows a specific circulation of performers and repertoire between north India and the Deccan, and it places qawwali within the same networks that transmitted Persian literary and Sufi culture southward.

III · The ShrineGulbarga

Bandanawaz and a lasting pilgrimage centre

Gulbarga became one of the principal institutional anchors of Deccan Sufism

Gisu Daraz, better known in the Deccan as Bandanawaz, settled in Gulbarga after leaving north India and became one of the most important transmitters of Sufi traditions to the plateau. His tomb and hospice developed into a major pilgrimage centre supported by successive Deccan dynasties.[2]

That institutional continuity matters for music. Qawwali in the Deccan is not only a courtly inheritance; it is also part of the living ritual ecology of dargahs, where poetry, pilgrimage, remembrance and communal gathering intersect. The shrine is therefore a more durable home for the form than any single dynasty.

IV · Two WorldsDurbar & Dargah

Courtly culture and shrine culture overlap

The Deccan’s musical history cannot be divided neatly into sacred and secular boxes

The Bahmani record shows royal patronage of qawwals, while Sufi sources place musical assemblies inside the devotional life of the khanqah and dargah. Those worlds overlapped: rulers patronised saints and musicians, Sufi centres attracted urban and courtly audiences, and the same Persianate literary repertoire could circulate across both settings.[1,2]

Dakhni.org therefore treats qawwali as a Deccan cultural form with several linked histories — religious, literary, urban and dynastic — rather than as a generic genre detached from the institutions in which it was performed.
