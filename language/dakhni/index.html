Dakhni · Dakhini · Dakani · Deccani — a southern literary and spoken form of Hindustani/Urdu

Dakhni is one modern spelling for the historic southern form of Hindustani/Urdu known in scholarship by several names. Encyclopaedia Iranica records Dakanī and Dakhinī as Deccan names for the language and notes that Hyderabadis still use the term for their distinctive form of Urdu. This page uses Dakhni as its house spelling while preserving the major variants readers will encounter in archives and scholarship.[1]

I · Names & ClassificationDakhni · Dakhini · Dakani · Deccani

One tradition, many spellings

Why the same language appears under several names

The historical language has never had a single Roman spelling. Dakhni, Dakhini, Dakani, Dakkani and Deccani all occur in modern writing. Encyclopaedia Iranica uses Dakanī and Dakhinī, describing the term as meaning ‘southern’ and identifying it as the Deccan form of Urdu/Hindustani.[1]

Those labels cover both a historical literary tradition and living regional speech. Treating Dakhni simply as either a separate language or merely a modern slang dialect can flatten that history: medieval Dakhni supported court poetry, Sufi devotional writing and extended prose, while modern Deccan varieties continue primarily in speech.

Contact with Telugu, Marathi and Kannada shaped vocabulary and usage in the south, while Persian and Arabic remained important literary sources. The precise boundaries between Dakhni, Hindavi, Rekhta and later standard Urdu changed over time, so this archive avoids presenting one modern language label as though it had been fixed for six centuries.[1]

II · Early WritingGulbarga · Fifteenth Century

From lingua franca to literary medium

Sufi networks and vernacular writing in the early Deccan

The movement of armies, administrators, migrants and Sufis from north India into the Deccan made a form of Hindavi useful as a lingua franca among communities speaking several Indo-Aryan and Dravidian languages. In the Deccan it acquired the local name Dakanī/Dakhinī and gradually developed a substantial religious and literary corpus.[1]

Khwaja Sayyid Muhammad Husayni Gesudaraz, better known as Bandanawaz (1321–1422), settled at Gulbarga around the turn of the fifteenth century. Vernacular devotional works have long been associated with him and his circle, although modern scholarship disputes the authorship and dating of some individual texts. That uncertainty matters: early Dakhni history is better described as an emerging textual tradition than as a single precisely dated ‘first book.’[2]

Sufi centres at Gulbarga and later other Deccan cities helped vernacular religious writing circulate beyond elite Persian-reading audiences. By the later sixteenth century the southern sultanates were actively patronising Dakhni poets and writers even while Persian remained central to administration.[1]

III · GolcondaGolconda & Hyderabad · 1580–1611

A sovereign-poet writes the Deccan into verse

Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah · r. 1580–1611

Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah, the fifth Qutb Shahi ruler, founded Hyderabad in 1591 and left a substantial body of poetry in Dakhni. His work is among the most important surviving evidence for the language as a mature courtly literary medium under the Deccan sultanates.[2]

His poetry joins Persianate forms and imagery to a recognisably Deccan setting: the city, monsoon, festivals, flowers, food and courtly life enter the verse alongside the conventional beloved, rose, wine cup and nightingale. That combination is one reason his divan is central to histories of Dakhni literature.

A well-known couplet associated with Muhammad Quli begins ‘Piya baaj pyaala piya jaye na / Piya baaj yek til jiya jaye na’ — a compact example of the idiom in which the Golconda court wrote and sang.

IV · BijapurBijapur · 1580–1627

The musical world of the Kitab-i-Nauras

Ibrahim Adil Shah II and the multilingual culture of Bijapur

At Bijapur, Ibrahim Adil Shah II (r. 1580–1627) patronised a court in which Persianate, Indic and Sufi cultural vocabularies interacted closely. The Kitab-i-Nauras, associated with his court and musical interests, is an important witness to that environment and to Dakhni's place in elite artistic life.[3]

The work invokes musical modes, devotional figures and courtly aesthetics across traditions. Rather than treating this mixture as proof of a uniquely harmonious age, it is more useful historically as evidence of the multilingual and multi-religious repertoire available to an early-modern Deccan court.

Together, Bijapur and Golconda show that Dakhni literature was not confined to one dynasty or city: by the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries it had multiple centres of patronage across the southern sultanates.[1]

V · Sab RasGolconda · c. 1635

Dakhni in extended prose

Mulla Wajhi and the literary possibilities of the vernacular

Mulla Wajhi's Sab Ras, completed at Golconda around the 1630s, is a major early work of extended Dakhni prose. Wajhi adapts a Persian allegorical tradition while giving the vernacular sustained narrative and rhetorical work to do.[2]

Its personified figures — Beauty, Love, Reason, the Glance and the Heart — belong to an established Persianate allegorical vocabulary, but the prose demonstrates that Dakhni had become capable of carrying long-form literary composition rather than only lyric or devotional fragments.

For researchers, Sab Ras is especially valuable because it preserves prose syntax and vocabulary that poetry alone cannot show. It is therefore a key text for studying the historical structure of Dakhni as well as its literature.

VI · Wali & the NorthLate Seventeenth–Eighteenth Century

When southern verse entered the Delhi literary world

Wali Deccani and a shifting centre of Urdu literary culture

By the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, poets associated with the Deccan were increasingly important to the vernacular literary culture of north India. Wali Deccani became especially influential as his poetry circulated in Delhi, where serious vernacular poetry was gaining a new elite audience.[4]

This did not mean that Dakhni simply ‘became Urdu’ at a single moment. Literary terminology, spelling and standards remained fluid, while patronage and prestige shifted increasingly toward Delhi and later Lucknow. The older southern tradition continued, but it no longer monopolised the prestige of vernacular Persianate poetry.

Siraj Aurangabadi (1715–1763) belongs to this later southern literary world. His work is often placed near the end of the classical Dakhni canon, while the spoken varieties of the Deccan continued independently of the northward shift in literary standardisation.

VII · Living DakhniModern Deccan

A literary inheritance that remains spoken

Hyderabad and the wider Deccan today

Dakhni survives most visibly today in speech across Hyderabad and other parts of the Deccan. Encyclopaedia Iranica specifically notes that inhabitants of Hyderabad continue to use Dakhinī for their distinctive form of Urdu.[1]

Modern Dakhni is heard in domestic conversation, humour, performance, film, qawwali and everyday city life. Its vocabulary and pronunciation vary by place and community, so there is no single spoken form that can stand for the entire Deccan.

Urdu's legal status is a separate question from Dakhni's linguistic identity. Telangana made Urdu the second official language throughout the state through the 2017 amendment to its Official Languages Act; that statute concerns Urdu as an official language and should not be read as a legal recognition of Dakhni as a separate official language.[5]

VIII · Finding DakhniResearch Guide

How to search the archive and scholarship

Spellings, key texts and starting points for further research

A search for only Dakhni will miss a large part of the scholarship. Researchers should also try Dakhini, Dakani, Dakkani, Deccani, Dakani Urdu and older terms such as Hindavi where the historical context warrants it. Iranica's bibliography is a useful gateway to linguistic scholarship, including work by D. J. Matthews and Ruth Laila Schmidt.[1]

For literary study, useful anchor texts include the poetry of Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah, Ibrahim Adil Shah II's Kitab-i-Nauras, Mulla Wajhi's Sab Ras, Wali Deccani and Siraj Aurangabadi. Rekhta's Deccani-adab collection provides a searchable route into digitised editions and later scholarship under several spelling variants.[6]

Within Dakhni.org, this page should be read together with the Urdu overview and the Language & Poetry heritage section. The aim is to separate what is documented from what is traditional, and to make alternate spellings discoverable rather than treating them as competing identities.

A Chronology of Dakhni

From medieval Deccan lingua franca to a living regional speech

  1. early 14th c.Delhi Sultanate expansion, migration and Sufi networks carry a north-Indian vernacular lingua franca into the multilingual Deccan.[1]
  2. 1347The Bahmani Sultanate is established with its first capital at Gulbarga, one of the principal early centres of the political and cultural world in which Dakhni developed.[2]
  3. c. 1399Khwaja Bandanawaz Gesudaraz settles at Gulbarga; vernacular devotional works are later associated with him and his circle, though individual attributions are debated.[2]
  4. 15th c.Dakanī/Dakhinī develops a growing corpus of religious and literary writing in the Deccan.[1]
  5. c. 1425–1429The Bahmani capital moves from Gulbarga to Bidar; the exact year is given differently in modern histories.
  6. late 16th c.The southern sultanates increasingly patronise Dakhni poets and writers while Persian remains important in administration.[1]
  7. 1591Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founds Hyderabad and becomes one of the best-known sovereign-poets associated with Dakhni.
  8. c. 1600Muhammad Quli's Dakhni poetry and the musical-literary culture of Ibrahim Adil Shah II's Bijapur illustrate multiple centres of high literary patronage.
  9. c. 1635Mulla Wajhi completes Sab Ras, a major early work of extended Dakhni prose.[2]
  10. late 17th–early 18th c.Southern vernacular poetry, especially the work associated with Wali Deccani, circulates in Delhi and contributes to the rapid growth of elite vernacular literary culture there.[4]
  11. 18th c.Delhi and later Lucknow become major centres of the literary standard increasingly known as Urdu, while Dakhni remains rooted in the south.
  12. 1918Osmania University is founded in Hyderabad with Urdu as its principal medium of higher instruction, reflecting the prestige of standard Urdu in the Asaf Jahi state.
  13. 1948The Asaf Jahi state ends; Dakhni continues as a spoken regional form across Hyderabad and the wider Deccan.
  14. 2017Telangana amends its Official Languages Act to make Urdu the second official language throughout the state; the law concerns Urdu and does not separately designate Dakhni.[5]
  15. todayDakhni/Dakhini remains a living spoken identity in Hyderabad and the Deccan while its historical literature is studied under multiple spellings and classifications.[1]
